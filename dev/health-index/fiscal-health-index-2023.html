Purpose

This document builds a fiscal health index for U.S. nonprofits from 2023 IRS Form 990 e-file data. It records every analytic decision made along the way and explains why it was made. It follows the strategy in dev/health-index/index-construction.rmd:

  1. Clustered indicators that move together define a small number of empirical dimensions.
  2. Standalone indicators are kept as individual measures rather than forced into a dimension.
  3. Noisy or redundant indicators are dropped from the measurement system.

The staged fiscal pipeline implements this strategy. Each stage returns an object you can inspect:

fiscal_diagnostics() -> fiscal_triage() -> fiscal_dimensions()
   -> fiscal_health_fit() -> fiscal_health_score() -> fiscal_weights() -> fiscal_health_index()

The software discovers structure, but people decide what to keep, which way each indicator points, and what the dimensions mean. Every one of those decisions is marked below as a Decision block with its reasoning. Summary of decisions at the end collects them in one table.

Key ideas

  • Dimension: a group of indicators that move together closely enough to be read as one underlying financial construct and scored as a single index.
  • Standalone indicator: a ratio that is kept in the measurement system but not forced into a dimension, because it shares little variance with the rest.
  • Orientation: the direction (“higher is healthier” or “lower is healthier”) applied to each indicator before indicators are averaged.
  • Rank-normal score: each value’s rank mapped onto a standard normal distribution. It keeps ordering and ignores outliers.
  • Parallel analysis: keep only the components whose eigenvalues beat those from random data of the same size.
  • Congruence: a similarity score between two loading matrices. At 0.95 or above, the dimensions are effectively the same.

Decision 1: Restrict the sample to full Form 990 filers with positive revenue, expenses, and total assets.

Why: Most fiscal health ratios need line items that only appear on the full Form 990: Part IX functional expenses, and Part X detail on cash, payables, fixed assets, and net-asset classes. 990-EZ filers would enter with structural zeros that look like real values. Mixing them in would mostly separate form type rather than financial condition. Positive denominators keep the core ratios (margins, return on assets, months of spending) defined for every organization. As a result, the index describes organizations large enough to file the full 990 (roughly $200k+ in gross receipts or $500k+ in assets). It does not describe the smallest nonprofits.

2. Correcting indicator inputs

Before choosing indicators, we checked whether each candidate ratio measures what it claims to. Three problems turned up, and each changes what goes into the model.

2.1 Net-asset classes for filers that do not follow SFAS 117

Part X lines 27–28 (net assets without/with donor restrictions) apply only to organizations that follow SFAS 117 / ASC 958. Other filers report equity on lines 30–32 (capital stock, paid-in surplus, retained earnings). For those filers, unrestricted net assets read as zero even when total net assets are large. Every reserve metric built on unrestricted net assets then understates their reserves.

unr <- zero(d$F9_10_NAFB_UNRESTRICT_EOY)

non_sfas <- unr == 0 & res == 0 & tot != 0

Decision 2: When lines 27–28 are both zero, treat total net assets as unrestricted.

Why: About 25% of the sample would otherwise score as having no discretionary reserves. 3. Candidate indicator pool

The package registry (fiscal_metrics()) lists 48 metrics. An index needs indicators that are directional (more is clearly better or clearly worse), scale-free (not a dollar amount), defined for nearly everyone, monotone in health, and not algebraically identical to another indicator. The table below screens every metric against those criteria.

Decision 4: Screen out metrics that are directional in name only. 12 indicators enter the model: cash_assets, days_cash_ops, liquid_assets_months, debt_assets, surplus_margin, return_assets, profit_predepr, self_suff, prog_exp, expenses_admin, op_reserve, assets_rev.

Why:

  • Composition measures (revenue mix, expense mix, asset mix) describe a business model, not financial condition. A high government-grant share is neither healthy nor unhealthy on its face.
  • Dollar levels would make the index mostly about size.
  • Structural missingness: current ratios are undefined for the ~40% of filers with no payables. Estimating dimensions on complete cases would quietly drop the simplest balance sheets.
  • Signed net-asset denominators make ratios non-monotone. An insolvent organization with negative net assets gets a negative debt-to-net-assets ratio and would rank as the least leveraged.
  • Algebraic identities make the correlation matrix singular. That breaks KMO and factor estimation and inflates a construct’s apparent weight.

Borderline cases (self_suff, assets_rev, prog_exp) are registry-directional but arguably context-dependent. They enter the pool so the data can show how they behave, and they are revisited at triage.

4. Transformation: rank-normal scores

Nonprofit ratios have extreme tails. Near-zero denominators and data-entry errors produce values in the thousands. The package’s _z columns winsorize and then apply a fitted normalizing transform. Correlation-based modeling, though, needs the Pearson correlations between transformed indicators to preserve the monotone association in the raw data. We checked whether they do.

rank_normal <- function(x) {
  x[!is.finite(x)] <- NA
  r <- rank(x, na.last = "keep", ties.method = "average")
  qnorm((r - 0.5) / sum(!is.na(x)))
}

Decision 5: Model rank-normal (van der Waerden) scores of the raw ratios, not the package _z columns.

Why: For several pairs, the _z columns shrink strong raw associations toward zero (compare spearman_raw with pearson_pkg_z). Some _z columns are far from standardized (means well away from 0, heavy tails). Rank-normal scores are bounded, symmetric, and insensitive to outliers. Their Pearson correlations track the raw Spearman correlations, so the correlation matrix that PCA sees reflects the real monotone structure in the data.

Cost: The transform depends on the reference distribution. To score a later year on the 2023 scale, map each value to its position in the 2023 distribution (for example with ecdf()) rather than re-ranking within the new year.

What the diagnostics show:

  • Two tight clusters. The reserve measures (days_cash_ops, op_reserve, liquid_assets_months) correlate strongly with each other, as do the performance measures (surplus_margin, profit_predepr, return_assets). debt_assets is negatively related to reserves.
  • Near-duplicates. Some pairs exceed the redundancy cutoff (|r| >= 0.90).
  • Weak connectors. prog_exp, expenses_admin, and self_suff have very low communality: they share almost no variance with the financial-condition measures.
  • Low-KMO asset-composition measures. cash_assets and assets_rev have individual KMO below 0.50. They relate to the reserve cluster inconsistently: assets_rev rises with reserves, while cash_assets is unrelated to liquid months of reserves. The two are strongly negatively correlated with each other (r = -0.48). That pattern points to a contrast between cash-based and asset-heavy organizations, not a shared health signal.

Decision 6: Keep the asset-composition measures (cash_assets, assets_rev) out of dimension estimation (review).

Why: Both fall below the conventional KMO adequacy floor (0.26 and 0.44). Substantively, their “healthy” direction depends on the organization’s model:

  • cash_assets: A high cash share of assets marks a small organization with no property or investments. An endowed university holds most of its liquidity in securities and so has a low cash share.
  • assets_rev: The registry treats this as lower is better (asset efficiency). In the data it loads positively with reserves, because endowed and capital-rich organizations have high asset-to-revenue ratios. Oriented by the registry, it would cancel against the reserve indicators.

The unreviewed solution above shows the consequence. Parallel analysis retains 3 components. debt_assets and assets_rev cross-load, and a separate component forms around cash_assets, set against debt_assets and assets_rev. That component separates cash-based organizations from capital-intensive ones: a difference in business model, not in condition.

Decision 7: Represent the performance cluster with surplus_margin, not profit_predepr.

Why: The automatic rule keeps the member with the higher KMO. Here the two differ only in the third decimal place, so the choice falls to substance. Surplus margin is the bottom line reported on Part I, line 19. It is the standard nonprofit operating-margin measure, and depreciation is a real cost of sustaining capacity.

Accepted automatic recommendations:

  • liquid_assets_months over op_reserve (r >= 0.90). Liquid unrestricted net assets add back mortgage debt on property, so they don’t treat debt-financed buildings as spent reserves. This matches the “months of liquid unrestricted net assets” standard in sector benchmarking.
  • expenses_admin as noise. It has low KMO, low communality, and weak connectedness.
  • prog_exp and self_suff as standalone. Their role in the composite is decided in Section 9.

Decision 8: Retain two dimensions.

Why:

  • Parallel analysis keeps 2 components: only those eigenvalues exceed what random data of the same size produce.
  • Simple structure. The two-component solution has no cross-loadings, and every indicator loads above 0.60 on exactly one component.
  • The one-component solution loads everything in the expected direction. That is evidence a general health factor exists and that a composite is meaningful. But it explains less than half the variance and hides the distinction between stocks and flows (Section 8).
  • The three-component solution just splits debt_assets off as a singleton. A one-indicator “dimension” is not a latent construct, and its eigenvalue sits below the parallel-analysis threshold.
  • EFA agrees. Common-factor analysis, which models only shared variance, recovers the same two groups.

7.3 Stability

A dimension worth naming should reappear in independent halves of the data and in organizations of very different size. We use Tucker’s congruence coefficient between loading matrices. Values of 0.95 or higher mean the factors are effectively equal; 0.85–0.94 means fair similarity.

match_congruence <- function(A, B) {
  C <- psych::factor.congruence(A, B)
  # align components by best absolute match
  diag(C[, apply(abs(C), 1, which.max), drop = FALSE])
}

##     "reserves_solvency" "operating_performance"

The labels come from what the grouped indicators have in common: their accounting source, their time horizon, and the financial question they answer. Dimensions are matched to labels by indicator membership, not by component number, so the labels stay correct if the component order changes on re-estimation.

Dimension A: Reserves and Solvency (reserves_solvency)

Indicator Sign What it measures
liquid_assets_months + Liquid unrestricted net assets (net of property, plus mortgage debt) in months of spending
days_cash_ops + Cash, savings, and receivables in days of cash operating expenses
debt_assets − Share of assets financed by liabilities

Why this label.

  • Same source and horizon. All three are balance-sheet stocks measured at year end. None depends on this year’s revenue or surplus.
  • Same question. How much cushion does the organization have, and how much of it is already claimed? The two reserve measures express the cushion in units of spending (months or days it could keep operating). Debt-to-assets loads negatively because liabilities are claims against that cushion: a leveraged organization’s apparent assets are less available to absorb a shock.
  • Why “solvency” is in the name. Parallel analysis did not support separating leverage from liquidity (the three-component solution only splits off a singleton). Keeping debt_assets in the label makes clear the dimension covers both having reserves and not owing them away.

Interpretation. High scores mean financial resilience: enough liquid, unencumbered equity to ride out a revenue disruption, delayed reimbursement, or unexpected cost. Low scores mean thin or negative liquid reserves and heavy reliance on borrowed resources. Such organizations are vulnerable even in a year with a surplus. This dimension changes slowly; it is the accumulated result of past years.

Dimension B: Operating Performance (operating_performance)

Indicator Sign What it measures
surplus_margin + Revenue less expenses, as a share of revenue
return_assets + Revenue less expenses, as a share of total assets

Why this label.

  • Same source and horizon. Both are income-statement flows for the current year. They share a numerator (the annual surplus or deficit) and differ only in denominator (revenue vs. assets). Their loadings reflect that shared numerator.
  • Why not “profitability.” That term implies profit maximization, which is not a nonprofit objective. “Operating performance” describes whether the year’s activities covered their full cost, including depreciation, which is the quantity lenders, funders, and boards monitor.

Interpretation. High scores mean the organization covered its costs this year with room to spare, which is the source of future reserves. Low scores mean an operating deficit. A single deficit year is not distress, and deficits can be deliberate spend-downs of prior surpluses. Persistent low scores erode Dimension A.

How the two dimensions relate

cat("Correlation between the two components (oblimin, full sample):",
    fmt(psych::principal(X, nfactors = 2, rotate = "oblimin")$Phi[1, 2], 2), "\n")
## Correlation between the two components (oblimin, full sample): 0.31

The dimensions are positively but modestly correlated. That matches the stock–flow logic: surpluses build reserves over time, but in any one year many organizations have strong balance sheets and a deficit, or the reverse. This modest correlation is why the index reports both dimension scores next to the composite. Two organizations with the same composite can have very different profiles: a well-reserved organization spending down savings, or a thinly capitalized one running surpluses.

Decision 10: Weight the composite 50/50 across the two dimensions, and keep the standalone indicators (prog_exp, self_suff) out of it.

Why equal weights: No external outcome in a single cross-section can calibrate weights (for example, later closure or insolvency). The two dimensions carry similar variance, and equal weights are the transparent default that is easiest to defend. fiscal_weights() accepts custom weights once panel outcomes are available to calibrate against.

Why exclude the standalones: With equal weighting, each standalone would get as much weight as a whole dimension.

  • prog_exp has communality near zero: it carries almost no information about reserves or results. Its “higher is better” reading is contested, since chronic underinvestment in administration is a documented risk factor (the “nonprofit starvation cycle”).
  • self_suff (program revenue ÷ expenses) describes the revenue model. A donor-funded organization with low self-sufficiency is not less healthy.

Both are still scored (fh_prog_exp, fh_self_suff) and returned for descriptive use.

Retained dimensions.

  • Reserves & Solvency (balance-sheet stocks): liquid reserves and cash runway, net of leverage. It measures resilience, the capacity to absorb shocks.
  • Operating Performance (income-statement flows): surplus relative to revenue and assets. It measures whether current operations are sustainable.

Limitations and next steps

  • One cross-section. Stability across tax years, and predictive validity (does a low score precede closure, filing lapses, or deficits?), need the multi-year panel. Scoring later years on the 2023 scale needs a reference-quantile version of the rank-normal transform.
  • Monotone scoring. Every indicator is treated as better-when-higher (or lower), with no ceiling. Adding an “excess reserves” or target-range rule, for example a penalty above a benchmark number of months of reserves, would be a substantive policy choice, and it is left to users.
  • Coverage. Organizations filing 990-EZ or 990-N are out of scope. A 990-EZ-compatible variant could use the Part I and Part II fields shared by both forms.
  • Industry context. Reserve norms differ by subsector: hospitals and universities differ from arts groups or food banks. The index is comparable across the sector as defined here. NTEE-relative percentiles (via the BMF merge) would add within-field comparisons.
  • Package fixes to follow up:
    • get_overhead_ratio() numerator
    • get_cash_assets_ratio() numerator
    • the SFAS 117 net-asset gap in reserve metrics
    • the fundr_eff registry direction
    • the days_cash_inv / current-ratio use of F9_10_ASSET_INV_SALE_EOY (inventories)
    • the _z normalization, which distorts correlations
