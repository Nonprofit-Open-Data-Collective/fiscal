Purpose

The original H5 (h5-surplus-allocation.Rmd) asked where a surplus goes: it regressed each balance-sheet block’s movement on the level of the surplus and read the coefficients as cents per surplus dollar. That document stands. This one asks a different and, for the index, more useful question:

When an organization has an unusually good year, what does it do with the money?

The difference matters because a surplus is already the residual of a decision. By the time a dollar shows up as surplus, the organization has decided not to spend it. Starting from the revenue windfall instead puts the spend-or-keep decision inside the estimate, which is where the interesting behaviour is. Every dollar of revenue arriving above what an organization normally raises is either spent in the same year or it is not; if it is not, it lands on the balance sheet as cash, receivables, property, investments, or debt paid down.

This is the mirror of H6. The two documents share one data preparation (baseline-prep.R), one population, one deflator and one baseline, so the upside and downside estimates are directly comparable — section 11 puts them in one table.

What is inherited from the H6 rework

  1. Windfalls are measured against a trailing three-year average, not year t-1. Against t-1, an organization recovering from one bad year looks identical to one receiving a windfall. Section 2 shows that almost a third of the year-over-year “windfalls” are exactly that.
  2. Everything is in constant 2023 dollars (CPI-U), applied before any baseline is formed.
  3. Every channel is reported twice: cents per dollar of windfall, and the same dollars as a share of what that channel started with. Putting 25 cents into cash and 25 cents into property is the same number twice, but it may double the cash and add 2% to the buildings.

A windfall is an organization-year in which total real revenue comes in at least 20% above the organization’s own inflation-adjusted average of the 3 prior tax years — the exact mirror of H6’s shock.

Scaling. Everything is divided by baseline expenses, the trailing three-year real average. Current-year expenses are an outcome here (spending the windfall is one of the things being measured), so they cannot be the denominator.

Sign convention. Throughout this document a positive number is a use of the windfall: spending above the normal level, an asset that grew by more than it normally does, or debt that grew by less than it normally does. On the revenue side a positive number is a source: a line that came in above its own baseline.

A 20% windfall sits 0.71 robust standard deviations above the median and occurs in 25.2% of organization-years — marginally more common than a 20% shortfall (23.3%). As on the downside, the same fixed percentage is a much weaker filter for small organizations than for large ones.

The upside tail is far fatter than the downside tail, and this is the one place where the two sides cannot be treated symmetrically. A shortfall is bounded: revenue cannot fall more than 100% below baseline without going negative, and only 0.34% of organization-years do. There is no corresponding ceiling.

Where to put the ceiling. The mirror of H6’s -1.0 bound in robust standard deviations is about 0.99, so the main estimate caps the windfall at 1.00 — revenue at most double its baseline. This is a much bigger exclusion than on the loss side: it removes 20.3% of windfalls, against 1.5% of shocks at the matching bound. That asymmetry is not a nuisance to be tuned away — it says that organizations which more than double their revenue relative to baseline are not having a good year, they are changing scale, and averaging them in with ordinary windfalls would let a few hundred capital campaigns set the allocation for everyone. Section 12 reports the estimate at other ceilings.

Was the year-over-year rule picking up recoveries?

build_channels <- function(z) {
  z <- copy(z)
  z[, dol_gain := R - base_R][, s_gain := dol_gain / base_E]
  for (i in seq_len(nrow(CH))) {
    r <- CH[i]
    d <- if (r$sign == 1) z[[r$actual]] - z[[r$base]] else z[[r$base]] - z[[r$actual]]
    z[[paste0("dol_", r$name)]] <- d
    z[[paste0("s_",   r$name)]] <- d / z$base_E
    z[[paste0("cap_", r$name)]] <- z[[r$capacity]]
  }
  z[, dol_keep := dol_gain - dol_spend][, s_keep := dol_keep / base_E]   # the residual: kept
  z[, dol_na := d_na - base_d_na][, s_na := dol_na / base_E]
  z[]
}

Outliers are handled by dropping organization-years, not clipping values, for the reason H5 and H6 both give: clipping each component separately breaks the adding-up the design rests on. Three rules apply, within year — the 1st/99th percentile of the scaled windfall, the 1st/99th of the net balance-sheet movement, and the top 1% of the largest single block movement (blocks offset each other, so an extreme swing between two of them passes the first two filters while carrying enormous leverage).

trim_sample <- function(z, trim = TRUE, bound = params$max_gain) {
  if (!is.null(bound)) z <- z[gap <= bound]
  if (!trim) return(z[])
  z[, keep := {
    qg <- quantile(s_gain, c(0.01, 0.99), na.rm = TRUE)
    qa <- quantile(s_na,   c(0.01, 0.99), na.rm = TRUE)
    qb <- quantile(max_abs_blk, 0.99, na.rm = TRUE)
    s_gain >= qg[[1]] & s_gain <= qg[[2]] & s_na >= qa[[1]] & s_na <= qa[[2]] &
      max_abs_blk <= qb
  }, by = year]
  z <- z[keep == TRUE]; z[, keep := NULL]; z[]
}
windfalls <- function(years) trim_sample(p[year %in% years & windfall == TRUE])

share_of_avg <- function(s, nm) mean(s[[paste0("s_", nm)]]) / mean(s$s_gain)
pct_capacity <- function(s, nm) {
  cap <- sum(s[[paste0("cap_", nm)]])
  if (!is.finite(cap) || cap <= 0) return(NA_real_)
  sum(s[[paste0("dol_", nm)]]) / cap
}
marginal <- function(s, nm) {
  f <- ols(s[[paste0("s_", nm)]], cbind(intercept = 1, gain = s$s_gain))
  c(b = f$b[["gain"]], se = f$se[["gain"]])
}
channel_table <- function(s, names_vec) {
  rbindlist(lapply(names_vec, function(nm) {
    m <- marginal(s, nm)
    data.table(channel = CH[name == nm, label],
               cents_per_dollar = share_of_avg(s, nm),
               pct_of_capacity  = pct_capacity(s, nm),
               marginal = m[["b"]], se = m[["se"]])
  }))
}

5. First-line allocation: spent or kept

The identity revenue - expenses = surplus means a windfall must show up either as spending above the organization’s own baseline, or as a surplus above its baseline. The two sum to exactly 1.

  • Extra spending = actual expenses minus baseline expenses. Positive means the organization spent more than its own three-year norm — the windfall was used this year.
  • Kept = the remainder, which lands on the balance sheet.

The first two columns must agree; they are the same quantity computed two ways. The third need not — net assets also move with unrealized investment gains and donated capital, which do not pass through the surplus.

The two columns weight organizations differently, and on this table they can disagree in sign. cents_per_dollar is a ratio of means of scaled channels, so each organization enters in proportion to its own baseline expenses — a small organization and a large one count roughly alike. pct_of_capacity is a ratio of raw dollar totals, so it is dominated by the largest organizations in the sample. Cash shows the effect plainly: the cents-per-dollar figure is positive (0.061) while the aggregate capacity figure is slightly negative (-0.003). Both are correct. Read together they say that the typical organization puts a little of a windfall into cash, while the large organizations that hold most of the sector’s cash were running their balances down over 2022-2023 faster than their own baseline growth — the tail end of the COVID cash build-up. Where the two columns disagree, the cents-per-dollar figure is the behavioural one and the capacity figure is the sector aggregate.

The same caveat H6 records applies to the investments row: Part X is fair value, so that block mixes deliberate investment of the windfall with market revaluation that would have happened anyway. Read cash, receivables, property and debt as behaviour; read investments as behaviour plus the market.

7. Is the windfall concentrated in one revenue stream?

This is the question the rework was asked for. A windfall can arrive as one large grant or as a generally good year across every line, and there is no reason to expect those to be treated the same way.

Measure. For each windfall organization-year, take the seven component deviations from baseline, keep the positive ones, and compute the largest one’s share of their total. A value near 1 means essentially all of the good news came from a single stream.

8. Windfalls concentrated in a single revenue stream

Section 7 asks whether concentration matters. This section asks which stream.

Definition. A component windfall is an organization-year in which one component comes in at least 33% above its own three-year baseline, and that rise is worth at least 10% of the organization’s baseline total revenue.

Denominator. As in H6’s mirror section, each component is scaled by its own rise, not by the total windfall. When one line jumps, total revenue does not necessarily rise by as much — other lines can fall at the same time, and crowding-out of that kind is one of the things worth measuring. The three shares below sum to exactly 1:

  • Offset by other revenue falling — the share of the rise cancelled by other lines coming in below their own baselines.
  • Spent — the share matched by spending above baseline.
  • Kept — the remainder, onto the balance sheet.
comp_flag <- function(z, nm) {

  ok <- is.finite(base_c) & is.finite(rise) & base_c > 0
  ok & (rise / base_c) >= params$comp_share & (rise / z$base_R) >= params$comp_material
}

The spread across components is much wider than anything in the aggregate, and it maps onto H6’s loss-side result line for line.

  • Program revenue is spent, almost entirely. 0.887 of a dollar of extra program revenue is matched by spending above baseline and only 0.020 is kept. H6 found the mirror: 0.85 of a lost program dollar was matched by cutting. Program revenue and program cost move together in both directions, which is what you would expect if they are two sides of one activity rather than a financing decision.
  • Contributed income is where discretion lives. Other contributions keep 0.358 per dollar and government grants 0.141, against near zero for program revenue.
  • Half of some windfalls never reach the bottom line. Other revenue is 50% offset by other lines falling, fundraising events 44%, membership dues 41%. These lines tend to move when something else is moving the other way, so the “windfall” is substantially a substitution.
  • The investments row is mechanical and should not be read as behaviour. A windfall in the investments line is mostly realized gains on asset sales, and selling an appreciated asset is exactly what makes the investments block on the balance sheet fall. That is why its balance-sheet coefficient is large and negative (-1.389 per dollar): the money did not go into investments, it came out of them and reappeared as revenue. The same accounting runs through the -0.527 debt figure.

flow_figure <- function(sources, uses, use_capacity_label, title, subtitle, dropped = NULL) {
  op <- par(mar = c(0.4, 0.4, 3.0, 0.4), bg = "white", family = "sans")
  on.exit(par(op))
  gap <- 0.045; centre <- 0.5
  lab_s <- sprintf("%s  %.2f", names(sources), sources)
  lab_u <- sprintf("%s  %.2f", names(uses), uses)
  sources_n <- sources / sum(sources); uses_n <- uses / sum(uses)
  half <- function(v) (sum(v) + gap * (length(v) - 1)) / 2
  top_max <- centre + max(half(sources_n), half(uses_n), 0.5)
  bot_min <- centre - max(half(sources_n), half(uses_n), 0.5)
  head_y <- top_max + 0.085; arrow_y <- bot_min - 0.105
  legend_y <- arrow_y - 0.075
  foot_y <- legend_y - (if (length(dropped)) 0.185 else 0.115)
  plot.new(); plot.window(xlim = c(0, 1), ylim = c(foot_y - 0.02, head_y + 0.06))
  stack <- function(v) {
    y <- centre + half(v)
    out <- lapply(v, function(h) { r <- c(top = y, bot = y - h); y <<- y - h - gap; r })
    names(out) <- names(v); out
  }
  ribbon <- function(x0, x1, t0, b0, t1, b1, col, alpha) {
    t <- seq(0, 1, length.out = 80); s <- t * t * (3 - 2 * t); xs <- x0 + (x1 - x0) * t
    polygon(c(xs, rev(xs)), c(t0 + (t1 - t0) * s, rev(b0 + (b1 - b0) * s)),
            col = adjustcolor(col, alpha), border = NA)
  }
  chevron <- function(x, ytop, ybot, col) {
    ymid <- (ytop + ybot) / 2; h <- min(0.020, (ytop - ybot) / 2.6); w <- 0.012
    if (h <= 0.003) return(invisible())
    lines(c(x - w, x, x - w), c(ymid + h, ymid, ymid - h), col = col, lwd = 1.6)
  }
  xs <- c(0.250, 0.300); xt <- c(0.487, 0.537); xu <- c(0.700, 0.750)
  srcb <- stack(sources_n); useb <- stack(uses_n); top <- centre + 0.5
  place <- function(boxes, minsep) {
    y <- vapply(boxes, mean, 0); n <- length(y)
    if (n > 1) for (i in (n - 1):1) if (y[i] - y[i + 1] < minsep) y[i] <- y[i + 1] + minsep
    y
  }
  ys_lab <- place(srcb, 0.052)
  yu_lab <- place(useb, if (any(nzchar(use_capacity_label))) 0.088 else 0.052)
  leader <- function(x0, x1, y_bar, y_lab, col) {
    if (abs(y_lab - y_bar) < 0.004) return(invisible())
    lines(c(x0, x1), c(y_bar, y_lab), col = adjustcolor(col, 0.45), lwd = 0.7)
  }
  cur <- top
  for (i in seq_along(srcb)) {
    ribbon(xs[2], xt[1], srcb[[i]][["top"]], srcb[[i]][["bot"]], cur, cur - sources_n[[i]],
           pal[["navy"]], 0.30)
    chevron(0.398, srcb[[i]][["top"]], srcb[[i]][["bot"]], adjustcolor(pal[["navy"]], 0.85))
    cur <- cur - sources_n[[i]]
  }
  cur <- top
  for (i in seq_along(useb)) {
    ribbon(xt[2], xu[1], cur, cur - uses_n[[i]], useb[[i]][["top"]], useb[[i]][["bot"]],
           pal[["accent"]], 0.34)
    chevron(0.632, useb[[i]][["top"]], useb[[i]][["bot"]], adjustcolor(pal[["accent_ink"]], 0.85))
    cur <- cur - uses_n[[i]]
  }
  for (i in seq_along(srcb)) {
    rect(xs[1], srcb[[i]][["bot"]], xs[2], srcb[[i]][["top"]], col = pal[["navy"]], border = NA)
    leader(xs[1] - 0.004, xs[1] - 0.012, mean(srcb[[i]]), ys_lab[i], pal[["ink_soft"]])
    text(xs[1] - 0.016, ys_lab[i], lab_s[i], adj = c(1, 0.5), cex = 0.68, col = pal[["ink"]])
  }
  rect(xt[1], centre - 0.5, xt[2], top, col = pal[["accent"]], border = NA)
  for (i in seq_along(useb)) {
    rect(xu[1], useb[[i]][["bot"]], xu[2], useb[[i]][["top"]], col = pal[["pumpkin"]], border = NA)
    leader(xu[2] + 0.004, xu[2] + 0.012, mean(useb[[i]]), yu_lab[i], pal[["ink_soft"]])
    ym <- yu_lab[i]
    if (nzchar(use_capacity_label[i])) {
      text(xu[2] + 0.016, ym + 0.018, lab_u[i], adj = c(0, 0.5), cex = 0.68, col = pal[["ink"]])
      text(xu[2] + 0.016, ym - 0.020, use_capacity_label[i], adj = c(0, 0.5), cex = 0.56,

    } else {
      text(xu[2] + 0.016, ym, lab_u[i], adj = c(0, 0.5), cex = 0.68, col = pal[["ink"]])
    }

  text(xs[2], head_y, "WHERE THE EXTRA MONEY CAME FROM", adj = c(1, 0.5), cex = 0.60,

14. Findings

  • A windfall is mostly spent, and that is the sharpest contrast with H6. 0.676 of each windfall dollar is matched by spending above the organization’s own baseline and 0.324 is kept. On the loss side only 0.104 of a shortfall is matched by lower spending. Organizations adjust spending upward far more readily than downward.
  • What is kept goes to cash first. 0.061 per windfall dollar ends the year as cash, which is -0.3% of the cash the organization was holding when the year began.
  • A third of the year-over-year “windfalls” are recoveries. 31.0% of the organization-years the t-1 rule flags are rejected by the baseline rule; their prior year sat a median -30.6% below their own baseline. The contamination is larger on the upside than the the 25% H6 found on the downside.
  • Windfalls are overwhelmingly concentrated, but concentration is not what drives the response. The median windfall takes 91% of its good news from a single revenue line, and 51% of windfall organization-years are single-stream on the 90% test. Yet the spending share is flat across the three concentration groups (0.684 / 0.664 / 0.674). Concentration shifts which block the kept portion lands in — more cash when it is one stream, more property when it is broad — but not how much is kept.
  • Which stream it arrives in is what matters. Per dollar of extra program revenue, 0.887 is spent and 0.020 kept; per dollar of extra unrestricted contributions, 0.358 is kept. That is an order-of-magnitude difference in retention driven entirely by the source, and it mirrors H6’s loss-side finding that program revenue falls are absorbed by cutting while contributed falls land on the balance sheet.
  • Several “windfalls” are substitutions. Between a fifth and a half of the rise in dues, events, other revenue and government grants is offset by other lines falling in the same year, so it never reaches the bottom line at all.
