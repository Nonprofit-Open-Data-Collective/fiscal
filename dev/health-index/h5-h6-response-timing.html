Purpose

H5 (revisited) and H6 both measure the response to a revenue event in the same tax year as the event. Revenue arrives above baseline in year t, and spending in year t and the balance-sheet movement during year t are what gets estimated. That is a real limitation, and it cuts in an obvious direction: a grant that lands in November cannot be spent before the year closes, and an organization that loses a contract in year t may not be able to cut staff until year t+1. Whatever the same-year estimate reports, some of the response has to be somewhere else.

This document asks four things, for the windfall side and the shock side alike:

  1. How much of the response happens in the event year at all?
  2. What does the following year look like, measured against the same pre-event baseline?
  3. Does the allocation pattern a year later mirror the same-year pattern, or does money move between channels as time passes?
  4. What is the two-year total, pooling the event year and the year after?

What is held fixed. The baseline stays the one built from the three years before the event — tax years t-1, t-2, t-3. Year t+1 is measured against that same pre-event baseline, never against a baseline that has absorbed the event. This is the whole point: “expenses in t+1 were $X above what this organization used to spend” is the quantity of interest, and a rolling baseline would define it away.

The confounder that governs everything below. A revenue event is not a one-year impulse. Section 2 shows that most of a windfall is still present a year later. Where revenue stays elevated, higher spending in t+1 is not delayed deployment of last year’s money — it is an ordinary response to this year’s money. Sections 5 and 7 separate the two by conditioning on what revenue actually did in t+1.

# Deviation of a channel from the organization's own pre-event baseline,
# DIVIDED BY BASELINE EXPENSES. The scaling is not cosmetic: H5 and H6 report
# ratios of means of scaled channels, which weights organizations roughly
# equally. Taking the same ratio on raw dollars instead weights by size and is
# dominated by the largest filers -- it gave a same-year expense response of
# 0.42 against H6's published 0.10. Every quantity here is scaled first so the
# event-year column reproduces the source documents exactly; section 9 checks
# that it does.
dev <- function(s, nm, when = c("t", "f1")) {
  when <- match.arg(when)
  i <- SPEC[name == nm, which = TRUE]
  a <- if (when == "t") s[[SPEC$actual[i]]] else s[[paste0("f1_", SPEC$actual[i])]]
  b <- s[[SPEC$base[i]]]
  (if (SPEC$sign[i] == 1) a - b else b - a) / s$base_E
}
gain <- function(s, when = c("t", "f1")) {
  when <- match.arg(when)
  ((if (when == "t") s$R else s$f1_R) - s$base_R) / s$base_E
}
keep <- function(s, when = c("t", "f1")) gain(s, when) - dev(s, "spend", when)

# Event samples. `dir` is +1 for windfalls and -1 for shocks; the bound is the
# matching one from each source document.
event_sample <- function(dir, years = 2022:2023) {
  z <- p[year %in% years & has_lead == TRUE &
         (if (dir > 0) gap >= TH & gap <= params$max_dev
          else         gap <= -TH & gap >= -params$max_dev)]
  z[, s_dev := (R - base_R) / base_E]
  z[, s_na  := (d_na - base_d_na) / base_E]
  z[, keepf := {
    qd <- quantile(s_dev, c(0.01, 0.99), na.rm = TRUE)
    qa <- quantile(s_na,  c(0.01, 0.99), na.rm = TRUE)
    qb <- quantile(max_abs_blk,    0.99, na.rm = TRUE)
    qf <- quantile(max_abs_blk_f1, 0.99, na.rm = TRUE)
    s_dev >= qd[[1]] & s_dev <= qd[[2]] & s_na >= qa[[1]] & s_na <= qa[[2]] &
      max_abs_blk <= qb & max_abs_blk_f1 <= qf
  }, by = year]
  z <- z[keepf == TRUE]; z[, keepf := NULL]
  z[, gap_f1 := (f1_R - base_R) / base_R]
  z[]
}

2. Does the event persist into the following year?

This is the first thing to establish, because it determines what a lagged response can mean.

persist_row <- function(s, lab) {
  data.table(event = lab, n = nrow(s),
             scaled_deviation_year_t = mean(gain(s, "t")),
             scaled_deviation_year_t1 = mean(gain(s, "f1")),
             persistence = mean(gain(s, "f1")) / mean(gain(s, "t")),
             median_gap_t = median(s$gap),
             median_gap_t1 = median(s$gap_f1))
}

grp <- function(s, dir) {
  if (dir > 0)
    factor(ifelse(s$gap_f1 >= TH, "still a windfall",
           ifelse(s$gap_f1 > 0,  "partly retained", "back to baseline or below")),
           levels = c("still a windfall", "partly retained", "back to baseline or below"))
  else
    factor(ifelse(s$gap_f1 <= -TH, "still a shock",
           ifelse(s$gap_f1 < 0,   "partly recovered", "back to baseline or above")),
           levels = c("still a shock", "partly recovered", "back to baseline or above"))
}

Windfalls persist; shocks partly mend. 103.4% of the dollar value of a windfall is still there a year later, against 51.2% of a shortfall. In other words the typical “windfall” is not a one-off cheque at all — it is the first year of a higher revenue level. That is the single most important thing to hold onto when reading the lagged columns below: for most of the sample, year t+1 brings more money, not just the memory of last year’s money.

3. Windfalls: same year, following year, and the two together

Every column below is divided by the same denominator — the year-t revenue deviation — so the first two columns add to the third exactly. A value of 0.30 in the “following year” column means “an extra 30 cents of this channel moved in year t+1, per dollar of revenue that arrived above baseline in year t”.

The top row is the revenue deviation itself, which is 1.00 in the event year by construction and equals the persistence ratio a year later. It is the yardstick for everything under it: channels cannot be expected to sum to 1 in the lagged column, because there is more revenue than 1 to allocate.

timing_table <- function(s) {
  d0 <- mean(gain(s, "t"))
  rows <- rbindlist(lapply(SPEC$name, function(nm) {
    a <- mean(dev(s, nm, "t")) / d0
    b <- mean(dev(s, nm, "f1")) / d0
    data.table(channel = SPEC[name == nm, label], event_year = a,
               following_year = b, two_year_total = a + b,
               share_delayed = ifelse(abs(a + b) > 1e-9, b / (a + b), NA_real_))
  }))
  head_rows <- rbindlist(list(
    data.table(channel = "Revenue deviation",
               event_year = 1, following_year = mean(gain(s, "f1")) / d0,
               two_year_total = 1 + mean(gain(s, "f1")) / d0, share_delayed = NA_real_),
    data.table(channel = "Kept (surplus above baseline)",
               event_year = mean(keep(s, "t")) / d0,
               following_year = mean(keep(s, "f1")) / d0,
               two_year_total = (mean(keep(s, "t")) + mean(keep(s, "f1"))) / d0,
               share_delayed = NA_real_)))
  rbind(head_rows[1], rows[1:2], head_rows[2], rows[3:nrow(rows)])
}

chk <- function(s, tt) {
  data.table(
    check = c("event year: spending + kept = 1",
              "following year: spending + kept = persistence"),
    value = c(tt[channel == "Extra spending", event_year] +
                tt[channel == "Kept (surplus above baseline)", event_year],
              tt[channel == "Extra spending", following_year] +
                tt[channel == "Kept (surplus above baseline)", following_year]),
    should_be = c(1, mean(gain(s, "f1")) / mean(gain(s, "t"))))
}

The same-year estimate is not missing most of the story, but it is missing a meaningful part of it. Spending above baseline runs at 0.673 in the event year and 0.903 in the following year, so 57.3% of the two-year spending response falls outside the window H5 measures. But because 103.4% of the revenue is also still present, most of that later spending is being paid for by later revenue, not by last year’s.

4. Does the allocation pattern look the same a year later?

The table above holds the denominator fixed to measure cumulative response. To ask whether organizations allocate a windfall differently as time passes, each year has to be normalised by its own revenue deviation.

mirror_table <- function(s) {
  d0 <- mean(gain(s, "t")); d1 <- mean(gain(s, "f1"))
  rbindlist(lapply(SPEC$name, function(nm) {
    a <- mean(dev(s, nm, "t")) / d0
    b <- mean(dev(s, nm, "f1")) / d1
    data.table(channel = SPEC[name == nm, label],
               allocation_in_event_year = a, allocation_in_following_year = b,
               difference = b - a)
  }))
}

5. Isolating the one-off windfall

Sections 3 and 4 mix two different organizations: the one that got a single large cheque, and the one that stepped up to a permanently higher revenue level. Only the first can tell us about delayed deployment. Splitting on what revenue did in t+1 separates them.

by_persist <- function(s, dir) {
  d <- rbindlist(lapply(levels(s$pgroup), function(lv) {
    q <- s[pgroup == lv]
    if (nrow(q) < 200) return(NULL)
    d0 <- mean(gain(q, "t"))
    data.table(group = lv, n = nrow(q),
               median_gap_t1 = median(q$gap_f1),
               persistence = mean(gain(q, "f1")) / d0,
               spending_event_year = mean(dev(q, "spend", "t")) / d0,
               spending_following_year = mean(dev(q, "spend", "f1")) / d0,
               spending_two_year = (mean(dev(q, "spend", "t")) + mean(dev(q, "spend", "f1"))) / d0,
               cash_event_year = mean(dev(q, "cash", "t")) / d0,
               cash_following_year = mean(dev(q, "cash", "f1")) / d0)
  }))
  d
}

6. The two-year decomposition with the identity intact

For a decomposition whose shares sum to 1 — the form the source documents use — both the numerator and the denominator have to run over the same two years. Here the denominator is the revenue deviation cumulated over t and t+1, and the baseline is doubled to match.

two_year <- function(s) {
  den <- mean(gain(s, "t") + gain(s, "f1"))
  out <- rbindlist(lapply(SPEC$name, function(nm) {
    data.table(channel = SPEC[name == nm, label],
               cents_per_two_year_dollar = mean(dev(s, nm, "t") + dev(s, nm, "f1")) / den)
  }))
  rbind(out[1:2],
        data.table(channel = "Kept (surplus above baseline)",
                   cents_per_two_year_dollar = mean(keep(s, "t") + keep(s, "f1")) / den),
        out[3:nrow(out)])
}

The result here is the opposite of the windfall side, and it was not what I expected. The natural hypothesis is that cutting is slow — leases, payroll and programme commitments do not unwind inside a tax year — so the same-year model should understate it and year two should show the catch-up. It does not. The lagged spending response is 0.013 per dollar of the year-t shortfall, against 0.107 in the event year, so only 11.1% of the two-year cutting response falls outside H6’s window. Measured against each year’s own revenue deviation the picture is the same: the cut share falls from 0.107 to 0.026.

That aggregate hides a composition effect, and the split below is what actually answers the question.

By year two the shocked sample has divided into two populations moving in opposite directions. Organizations still below baseline (63% of the sample) do cut further — 0.181 in the following year on top of 0.143 in the event year. Organizations whose revenue recovered spend above baseline in year two (-0.680, negative because the sign convention makes cutting positive). The two nearly cancel, which is why the pooled lagged figure is close to zero.

So the honest answer to “does cutting catch up?” is: only for organizations whose revenue does not. There is no evidence of a general lagged retrenchment that the same-year model is missing — what looks like delay is recovery.

A second year widens the ratchet rather than closing it. In the event year 0.673 of a windfall is spent against 0.107 of a shortfall cut — a gap of 0.566. On the two-year decomposition, each side scaled by its own cumulative revenue deviation so the two are comparable, the figures are 0.775 and 0.079 — a gap of 0.695. Extra revenue keeps being spent in year two; missing revenue keeps being absorbed by the balance sheet. Whatever asymmetry the same-year estimate shows, waiting a year does not soften it.

The balance-sheet rows say where the difference is warehoused. Over two years a windfall leaves essentially nothing extra in cash (-0.003 per cumulative dollar) because what was banked in the event year is spent in the next, while a shortfall takes 0.424 out of cash and 0.452 out of investments and does not put it back. Two years after a shock the balance sheet is still carrying most of the loss; two years after a windfall it looks much as it did before, and the money has gone into the programme.

9. Robustness

Does the event year still reproduce H5 and H6?

The event-year column ought to be the published estimate. It cannot match to the digit, because this sample additionally requires a following year to exist and trims on that year’s block movements, so the check is whether the restriction moves the number.

published <- function(dir) {
  z <- p[year %in% 2022:2023 &
         (if (dir > 0) gap >= TH & gap <= params$max_dev
          else         gap <= -TH & gap >= -params$max_dev)]
  z[, s_dev := (R - base_R) / base_E][, s_na := (d_na - base_d_na) / base_E]
  z[, kp := {
    qd <- quantile(s_dev, c(0.01, 0.99)); qa <- quantile(s_na, c(0.01, 0.99))
    qb <- quantile(max_abs_blk, 0.99)
    s_dev >= qd[[1]] & s_dev <= qd[[2]] & s_na >= qa[[1]] & s_na <= qa[[2]] & max_abs_blk <= qb
  }, by = year]
  z <- z[kp == TRUE]
  list(n = nrow(z), b = mean(dev(z, "spend", "t")) / mean(gain(z, "t")))
}

10. Findings

  • Yes, everything in H5 and H6 is measured in the event year, and section 9 confirms the event-year column here reproduces both (windfall 0.673 against H5’s published 0.676; shock 0.107 against H6’s 0.104).
  • The two sides behave completely differently once a year is added. 57.3% of the two-year spending response to a windfall falls outside the event year, against only 11.1% of the cutting response to a shock. Spending has a long tail; cutting does not.
  • The lagged windfall response is mostly not delayed deployment, because the windfall itself persists. 103.4% of a windfall’s scaled value is still present a year later, against 51.2% of a shortfall’s. For the 7,516 organizations whose revenue did revert, year two is genuine deployment: they spend 0.103 above baseline while revenue is below it, funded by drawing cash down 0.541.
  • “Cutting is slow” is not supported. The obvious hypothesis — that the same-year model understates retrenchment because commitments take a year to unwind — fails. The aggregate lagged cut is 0.013. What that conceals is a split: organizations still below baseline in year two cut a further 0.181, while those that recovered expand. Cutting continues only where the revenue loss does.
  • Two years widens the ratchet. On each side’s own cumulative deviation, 0.775 of a windfall is spent against 0.079 of a shortfall cut. Two years after a shock the balance sheet is still carrying 0.921 of it.
  • The windfall’s cash bulge is temporary. Cash takes 0.064 per dollar in the event year and gives back 0.069 in the next, for a two-year total of -0.005. Property, by contrast, accumulates across both years (0.135). Money is parked liquid on arrival and then either spent or committed.
  • Caveat. 2023 events rely on 2024 filings, which are 88% complete and skew toward December filers; section 9 shows the 2022-only estimate, which moves the headline by about a point.
