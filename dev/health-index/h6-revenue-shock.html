Purpose

H5 asked where a surplus goes. This document asks the mirror question about the loss side: when revenue falls sharply, what absorbs the loss?

Every dollar of revenue an organization does not receive has to be found somewhere. It comes out of expenses, or it comes out of the balance sheet as a worse surplus — and that worse surplus is itself drawn from cash, receivables, property, investments, or new borrowing. The accounting identity pins the split down exactly, so the estimate is the split itself, not whether it exists.

What changed in this version, and why

The first version of this document defined a shock as revenue falling at least 20% below the prior year. That measure cannot tell a shock apart from the second half of a windfall. An organization that lands a large capital grant, a bequest, or a one-off capital campaign in year t-1 and returns to its normal level in year t records a >20% “drop” while nothing whatever has gone wrong. The v1 estimates were visibly contaminated by this: the headline said organizations raised expenses by 9 cents per dollar “lost”, which is what you would expect if a large share of the sample were spending down last year’s windfall rather than absorbing a loss.

Three changes follow from that diagnosis.

  1. The baseline is a trailing three-year average, not year t-1. A single windfall year moves a three-year average by about a third as much as it moves the prior-year level, so reversion to normal no longer registers as a shock. Section 3 measures how much of the v1 sample was reversion.
  2. Everything is in constant dollars. Over 2016-2024 the price level rose about 31%. A nominal baseline built from three older years is mechanically low, which biases the shock measure toward finding fewer shocks and understates each one. All dollar quantities are deflated to 2023 dollars by the CPI-U before any baseline is formed.
  3. Every channel is reported twice: as cents per dollar of shortfall, and as a share of what that channel had to give. Losing 25 cents from cash and 25 cents from investments is the same number twice, but it may be half the cash on hand and 3% of the investments. The second column is where absorptive capacity shows up.

Sections 6-9 keep the v1 estimands on the new shock definition. Section 10 is new: it isolates shocks to a single revenue component.

A shock is an organization-year in which total real revenue falls at least 20% below the organization’s own inflation-adjusted average of the 3 prior tax years.

Window. Shocks landing in tax years 2022 and 2023, December year-ends, organizations that filed a full 990 in every year 2019-2023. Because a baseline needs three prior filings, the panel was extended back to tax year 2016 (dev/panel/panel_build_baseline.R); that extension is what lets shock years 2019, 2020 and 2021 appear in the contrast table with a clean pre-COVID baseline.

Scaling. Everything is divided by baseline expenses — the same trailing three-year real average, applied to total expenses. Current-year expenses are one of the outcomes here, so an organization that responds by cutting deeply would shrink its own denominator and bias its expense-cut share toward zero. The baseline is fixed before the shock lands.

Three things follow, and they matter for how the threshold should be set.

  • A 20% shortfall is not a rare event. It sits -0.69 robust standard deviations below the median and selects 23.3% of all organization-years — roughly one year in four. Revenue at this population is simply very volatile relative to a three-year mean.
  • The threshold is not size-neutral. Volatility falls steeply with size: the robust SD is 0.561 for organizations under $100k and 0.171 for those above $10m. The same 20% rule is 0.33 SDs for the smallest band and 1.22 SDs for the largest, so it flags 34% of the smallest organizations against 15% of the largest. A fixed percentage cut point is, in effect, a small-organization filter.
  • What to do about it. Section 10 shows the absorption split is stable from a 10% threshold to a 50% one, so nothing in this document turns on the choice. But if the index is going to call something a “shock”, 20% against a three-year baseline is too weak a claim on its own, and a size-relative cut point — a fixed number of the organization’s own robust SDs — would flag a comparable share in every size band. That is a change to the index definition, not to this estimate, so it is flagged here rather than made.

Yes, and it should be at 1.00. Total revenue on Part VIII is a net figure: a large realized loss on an asset sale enters negatively and can push reported total revenue below zero, which makes the gap smaller than -1 without the organization having lost a single dollar of operating income. A gap of exactly -1 means “revenue this year was zero”; anything beyond that is an accounting artefact rather than a deeper shock, and because the gap is the denominator of every share in this document, those rows have unbounded leverage. Capping at -1 removes 2,279 organization-years (0.340% of the sample). The 1st/99th percentile trim in section 3 does most of the work in practice; the bound is a guard against the pathological tail rather than a live constraint.

3. Sample and trimming

Outliers are handled by dropping organization-years, not by clipping values — the same rule H5 arrived at. Winsorizing each component separately breaks the identity the whole design rests on: the clipped parts stop summing to the clipped total and the coefficients stop adding up.

A third rule is needed beyond the two H5 used. Trimming on the shortfall and on the net balance-sheet movement is not enough, because the blocks can offset each other: an organization-year with a colossal swing into investments and an equal swing out of cash has a small net movement and survives both filters while carrying enormous leverage over any single block’s coefficient. The scaled block channels reach six figures at their extremes. So organization-years in the top 1% of the largest absolute block movement are dropped as well. On the headline 2022-2023 estimate this changes almost nothing (it removes 79 rows and moves the expense-cut share by 0.002), but it is what makes the early shock years in section 10 readable at all — the 2019 investments channel goes from -14.0 to -0.12 once the handful of extreme rows are gone.

trim_sample <- function(z, trim = TRUE, bound = params$max_shock) {
  if (!is.null(bound)) z <- z[gap >= -bound]
  if (!trim) return(z[])
  # `:=` by group assigns back to the right rows; a grouped select would return
  # the flags in group order and misalign them when years are interleaved.
  z[, keep := {
    ql <- quantile(s_loss, c(0.01, 0.99), na.rm = TRUE)
    qa <- quantile(s_na,   c(0.01, 0.99), na.rm = TRUE)
    qb <- quantile(max_abs_blk, 0.99, na.rm = TRUE)
    s_loss >= ql[[1]] & s_loss <= ql[[2]] & s_na >= qa[[1]] & s_na <= qa[[2]] &
      max_abs_blk <= qb
  }, by = year]
  z <- z[keep == TRUE]; z[, keep := NULL]; z[]
}
shocked <- function(years) trim_sample(p[year %in% years & shock == TRUE])

4. Three ways to read the numbers

Three estimands answer different questions, and this document reports all three because they can disagree.

  • Cents per dollar of shortfall (the headline). The total dollars that flowed through a channel across the shocked sample, divided by the total dollars of shortfall. Because each channel is a component of the shortfall row by row, these shares sum to exactly 1. This is the quantity the figure shows.
  • Per cent of its own capacity. The same dollars, divided by what that channel had to give: the stock at the start of the year for a balance-sheet block, the baseline level for a revenue or expense line. This is the column that separates “drew a lot because it had a lot” from “drew down most of what it had”.
  • Marginal response (OLS). The slope of the channel on the size of the shortfall, within the shocked sample. This answers “a deeper shock is absorbed how differently”, which is not the same as “the average shock is absorbed how”. Standard errors are HC1.
# ols() is defined in baseline-prep.R (HC1 standard errors).
share_of_avg <- function(s, nm) mean(s[[paste0("s_", nm)]]) / mean(s$s_loss)
pct_capacity <- function(s, nm) {
  cap <- sum(s[[paste0("cap_", nm)]])
  if (!is.finite(cap) || cap <= 0) return(NA_real_)
  sum(s[[paste0("dol_", nm)]]) / cap
}
marginal <- function(s, nm) {
  f <- ols(s[[paste0("s_", nm)]], cbind(intercept = 1, loss = s$s_loss))
  c(b = f$b[["loss"]], se = f$se[["loss"]])
}
channel_table <- function(s, names_vec) {
  rbindlist(lapply(names_vec, function(nm) {
    m <- marginal(s, nm)
    data.table(channel = CH[name == nm, label],
               cents_per_dollar = share_of_avg(s, nm),
               pct_of_capacity  = pct_capacity(s, nm),
               marginal = m[["b"]], se = m[["se"]])
  }))
}

6. First-line absorption: expenses or the balance sheet

The identity revenue - expenses = surplus means a shortfall in revenue must show up either as expenses coming in below their own baseline, or as a surplus below its own baseline. These two shares sum to exactly 1. That is a check on the code, not a finding — if it fails, something upstream is wrong.

How to read the sign. Both columns are positive numbers, and both describe money that did not move as it normally would:

  • Expense cuts = baseline expenses minus actual expenses. Positive means the organization spent less than its own three-year norm — it cut. Negative would mean it spent more than normal despite the shortfall.
  • Shortfall run through the surplus = the rest. Positive means the surplus came in below its norm, which is to say the gap was carried on the balance sheet rather than closed by spending less.

A split of 0.60 / 0.40 therefore reads: of each dollar that did not arrive, 60 cents was matched by spending that did not happen, and 40 cents was absorbed by the balance sheet.

7. Where the balance sheet absorbs it

The surplus shortfall has to be drawn from somewhere, and the same identity as H5 applies: asset-side changes minus liability-side changes equal the change in net assets. Signs are set so that a positive number is a source of funds: an asset that grew less than normal (or shrank), or a liability that grew more than normal.

Each block enters as its movement during the shock year measured against its own baseline movement. An organization that normally adds $40k to cash a year and this year added nothing has found $40k, even though its cash did not fall. Comparing the movement to zero instead of to its baseline would miss that entirely.

The first two columns must agree: they are the same quantity computed two ways. The third need not — net assets also move with unrealized investment gains, prior-period adjustments, and donated capital, none of which pass through the surplus. The gap between them is that non-operating drift, and it is worth reading rather than dismissing.

The pct_of_capacity column is where the absorptive-capacity reading lives. Two blocks can contribute the same cents per dollar while one has given up a large fraction of its stock and the other a trivial one; only the second column distinguishes a block that is nearly exhausted from a block that has barely been touched. Cash and investments illustrate the point directly: they contribute roughly comparable cents per dollar, but cash gives up 27.0% of the stock it started with against 13.1% for investments. Cash is doing far more of the work relative to what there is of it.

The two columns weight organizations differently. cents_per_dollar is a ratio of means of scaled channels, so organizations enter roughly alike regardless of size; pct_of_capacity is a ratio of raw dollar totals and is therefore dominated by the largest organizations in the sample. On this table they agree in sign everywhere except other liabilities, where both figures are near zero, but the distinction matters when reading any single row: the cents-per-dollar column is the behavioural estimate and the capacity column is the sector aggregate. The companion document h5-windfall-allocation.Rmd has a case where the two genuinely disagree in sign.

One caveat on the investments block, which the year-by-year table in section 10 makes unavoidable. Part X reports assets at fair value, so the movement in “investments, other assets” mixes two different things: deliberate liquidation to cover a shortfall, and market revaluation that would have happened anyway. The sign convention cannot tell them apart, and in a year when markets fall, a portfolio that was never touched still registers as a large source of funds. That is most of why this channel swings from 1.130 in 2022 — a year markets fell sharply — to -0.082 in 2023. Read cash, receivables and borrowing as behaviour; read the investments row as behaviour plus the market, and prefer its pct_of_capacity column, which at least scales the movement by the portfolio it came from.

8. Do reserves buffer the cut?

This is the question the memo singled out. The reserves dimension of the index is interpreted as a buffer, which is a claim about behaviour within organizations: an organization with more months of cash on hand should be able to absorb a shock without cutting programs as deeply. If high- and low-reserve organizations cut the same way, the dimension is measuring size or sector, not resilience.

9. Shocks to a single revenue component

The sections above treat a shock as a fall in total revenue, whatever its source. But a government grant that ends, a membership base that erodes, and an investment portfolio that turns down are different events, and there is no reason to expect them to be absorbed the same way. This section isolates them.

Definition. A component shock is an organization-year in which one component falls at least 33% below its own three-year baseline, and that fall is worth at least 10% of the organization’s baseline total revenue. The first condition makes it a real disruption to that revenue stream; the second keeps it material to the organization overall. The total-revenue threshold is deliberately looser here (10% rather than 20%): requiring a 20% total-revenue shock as well would throw away every organization that lost a major funder and partly replaced it, which is precisely the interesting case.

The denominator has to change

Everywhere above, channels are expressed per dollar of total revenue shortfall. That denominator does not survive here, and the reason is the most interesting thing in this section: when one revenue line collapses, total revenue often does not fall. Organizations that lose a third or more of their membership dues make almost all of it up elsewhere, and organizations that lose their fundraising events end the year with total revenue above baseline on average. Dividing by a mean shortfall that is near zero, or negative, produces ratios that are arithmetically correct and completely meaningless — an early version of this table reported a -1.03 expense-cut share for event losses for exactly that reason.

So each component is scaled by its own dollar fall, and the first question asked is how much of that fall ever reached the bottom line:

  • Replaced by other revenue — the share of the lost dollars offset by other lines coming in above their own baselines.
  • Expense cuts — the share matched by spending below baseline.
  • Run through the surplus — the remainder, carried on the balance sheet.

These three sum to exactly 1 by the same identity used throughout, and the balance-sheet columns are on the same per-dollar-of-line-lost scale.

  • Program revenue behaves completely differently from everything else. 12. Findings

  • The split. Of each dollar of revenue that failed to arrive relative to a three-year inflation-adjusted baseline, 0.104 is matched by spending that did not happen and 0.896 is carried on the balance sheet.
  • What the old rule was measuring. 25.3% of the organization-years the prior-year rule called a shock are rejected by the baseline rule, and their median prior year sat 30.4% above their own baseline. The v1 sample was substantially a windfall-reversion sample.
  • Cash. 0.443 per dollar short comes out of cash, which is 27.0% of the cash these organizations were holding when the year began.
  • Borrowing. 0.020 per dollar short is funded by new identified debt.
  • Reserves buffer the cut, and the effect is large. The expense-cut share for low-reserve organizations is 0.393 against -0.105 for high-reserve organizations, which spend above baseline through the shock and draw 0.898 per dollar from cash instead — 48.2% of the cash they held. Within organizations the interaction agrees: a shock cuts expenses by 0.357, but high-reserve organizations cut 0.097 less and draw 0.341 more from cash. This is the behavioural claim the reserves dimension of the index rests on, and it holds.
  • Which line is lost matters far more than how much is lost. Of a dollar of lost program revenue, 0.848 is matched by lower spending; of a dollar of lost membership dues, 0.951 is simply replaced from other sources and the expense response is essentially zero. A single “revenue shock” dimension in the index averages over a genuine four-to-one difference in how organizations respond.
  • The 20% threshold is weaker than it sounds. A 20% shortfall against a three-year baseline is 0.69 robust standard deviations from the median and occurs in 23% of organization-years, and because volatility falls steeply with size it flags 34% of the smallest organizations against 15% of the largest. The absorption split is stable across thresholds, so no estimate here depends on it, but a size-relative cut point would be a better definition for the index.
